# Audit pass 6 — 2026-09-20

Twenty-seven fixes across sixty-two commits, found by ten premises rather than
by reading the code in order. Each premise is written down because the premise is the reusable part:
the bugs are gone, the questions still work. (The sections below count defects,
not commits, and come to more: some commits carry two -- the two dead
controls, the two step-mode clock defects -- and a few landed inside the
`test:` commit that caught them. Commit counts are from `git log`; defect
counts are the bullets.)

Everything here was measured in a browser before it was believed, and four
fixes were written, measured and thrown away. Those are in here too.

## 1. Coupled state that a handler updates halfway — 11 defects

The clock, the `paused` flag and the PGN `Result` are three pieces of state that
have to agree, and several handlers moved one without the others. `startSide` is
the only thing that sets `clock.since`, so anything that clears `paused` must
call it; anything that ends or holds a game must call `pauseClock`; and the
"Paused" badge only appears over a clock that has actually stopped, so a stopped
clock with `paused` false is invisible.

- **A takeback left the result behind.** `1. f3 e5 2. g4 Qh4#`, take it back and
  play on: the mate is a variation, the game continues, and the header still
  said `0-1`, so the PGN, the auto-save and the library all filed a game in
  progress as one Black had won. Only an ending ever wrote a `Result` and
  nothing cleared one. The clear is keyed to the main line's root id, so an
  imported `1-0` that no mate accounts for — a resignation, an adjudication —
  survives both a takeback and a result remembered from a previous game.
- **Switching mode froze the clock.** Pass and play has no Pause button, so
  Space is its pause. Changing mode afterwards dropped the pause flag without
  calling `startSide`: the badge went away and the clock of whoever was on move
  sat still until they played.
- **Resume restarted a finished game's clock**, undoing what `moveEndedGame`
  exists to prevent — a mated side's clock counting on will flag and turn
  "Checkmate" into "flagged on time".
- **Navigating did the same sideways.** `takebackMove` already knew that handing
  the turn back means handing the clock back, and fixed only its own door:
  pressing ← after 1. e4 in a 3+2 game left "White to move" over Black's clock
  counting down. Against the engine the clock was not held while the reader
  explored, draining whoever was on move — sometimes the engine's own.
- **Recorded clocks omitted the increment.** `[%clk]` is the reading *after* a
  move, and it was taken before the clock was pressed, so a 3+2 export sat two
  seconds under the clock the player had just watched. Later moves hid it
  because their baseline was short by the same amount; only each side's first
  move, measured against the starting bank, came out two seconds long.
- **The engine was charged for its own boot.** `isBoardInputLocked` does not
  look at engine readiness -- on your own turn the board takes your move
  whether or not the opponent exists yet -- and neither did anything that
  holds the clock. A timed game played the moment it starts handed the clock
  to an opponent that could not move and counted the WASM load against it.
  **Measured** with the handshake stopped one message short of ready: 2:59 to
  2:54 across five seconds. The mechanism, not the magnitude: a real boot is
  under a second on the single-threaded local build and seconds on the
  multi-threaded one, and `engineStartupTimeoutMs` allows 30s local and 120s
  for a CDN build before giving up on it.
- **And a released engine searched off the clock.** A hold stops the clock and
  `moveMade` pays an increment only for a move made *on* it, so every way of
  letting the engine go has to hand the clock back. `handleStep` did it for
  its own release and nothing did it for the others. The AI loop now starts
  the clock where it commits to a search, which is the one place they all
  reach; in an ordinary game that line is a no-op, because the clock is
  already running for that side.

- **Step mode held the engine's clock once per game.** Step is a study
  control -- the reader decides when the engine thinks -- so the engine must
  not be charged for the wait, and two places already said so, one of them
  recording the measurement that put the hold there. But Step sets `paused`
  after every engine move and the hold was written `... && !paused`, so it was
  true on the engine's first turn and false on every turn after: **measured**,
  held at 2:59 through five seconds on the first turn and 2:59 to 2:54 through
  five seconds on the second.
- **And letting go did not let go.** `handleStep` starts the clock and hands
  over; `isAiThinking` does not go up until the loop runs, and the hold
  re-fires in between, on the clock it was just given. The engine searched on a
  stopped clock, and `moveMade` pays an increment only for a move made *on* the
  clock -- which is the thing `handleStep` restarts it to protect. **Measured**:
  2:59 before the step and 2:59 after a move worth two seconds. Guarding on the
  step flag alone changed nothing, also measured: the AI loop is declared above
  that effect, so in the commit `handleStep` causes it runs first and clears the
  flag it was being read for.

- **And a replayed move kept the old one.** `addMove` de-dupes by UCI, which is
  right -- replaying a move that is already a variation makes it the game again
  rather than putting a second copy beside it -- but it did not take the new
  reading with it. A take-back refunds nothing, so the second attempt is always
  further down the clock: **measured** in a 3+2 game, 3:01 before the take-back,
  2:57 on the face after it, and 0:03:01 in the export. It hits the engine's
  reply as well as your own move, which is the commoner half of it: take a move
  back, play it again, and the engine repeating its answer re-uses that node
  too.

## 2. The app's idea of a platform concept against the platform's — 3 defects

- **Focus left an `aria-modal` dialog past a disclosure.** A `<details>`
  disclosure is focusable and carries no `tabindex`, so the trap had never heard
  of it: 25 controls the browser would tab to against 23 it knew, and one of the
  two it missed sat *after* the last one it knew. Expand the advanced options,
  collapse them, press Tab — `<body>`.
- **And left it again in Safari**, for a different reason. WebKit tabs to text
  fields and disclosures and skips every button, so those 25 controls are a
  three-stop cycle whose ends are nowhere near the list's first and last. Three
  presses of Shift+Tab reached the summary that opens the dialog. The fix
  catches focus that lands outside rather than seizing Tab, so each browser
  still steps where it likes.
- **The skip links could not be reached in Safari.** Same platform rule: Safari
  skips links too, so the first Tab stop was the Settings summary and the one
  mechanism for getting past the chrome was unusable. An explicit `tabindex` is
  what Safari wants; the `tabIndex` *property* already read 0.

## 3. Other engines — 2 defects

`UI_TEST_BROWSER=firefox|webkit` had been supported since the suite was written
and never once used. Both engines were already installed. Full write-up in
`cross-browser-2026-09-19.md`; the second defect was:

- **Safari threw away every dropdown's box.** Same element, same rules:
  computed `min-height` 18px against Chromium's 32px, padding 0 against
  5.76px 7.36px, drawn 18px tall at 100% text and 33px at 200% where Chromium
  draws 32 and 55. The selectors match in both engines; the rules do not survive
  a native menulist. So every sizing rule the app has for a dropdown was a no-op
  on the only engine iOS has, including the 44px touch target.

## 4. Double submission — 3 defects

React state is not a re-entrancy guard. `if (busy) return` where `busy` is
`useState` reads `false` for every click in the same tick, and the `disabled`
attribute is equally stale.

- **Library Save**: three clicks, three copies, auto-numbered "(2)" and "(3)" so
  the duplicates arrived looking deliberate.
- **Save review**: three identical runs. All six routines in that panel shared
  the flag, so the fix went to the guard rather than the button.
- **Database import**: a four-game file became **twelve**. The worst of the
  three, and the reason is a mitigation: the button waits two frames to paint
  "Adding…" before 3.1 seconds of parsing, and cannot disable until React has
  rendered that label — so the wait for the freeze *is* the window the second
  press lands in.

Correct patterns already in the repo, worth copying: fetches abort the previous
controller; loads use a monotonic sequence ref and check it after awaiting.

## 5. Features whose rules are tested and whose wiring is not — 1 defect

- **A blindfold a hover could take off.** The pieces are drawn transparent and
  the accessible names are deliberately left whole — enforcing a blindfold
  through the accessibility tree would take the board from a reader who never
  chose one. What fell between the two was `title`, which is not an
  accessibility affordance but a box the browser draws under the pointer.
  Measured: pieces at `opacity: 0`, every square carrying
  `title="e2, White pawn"`.

Premove, the threat probe, the hint and the tablebase were examined the same way
and are correct. The threat probe is worth a note: its whole trick is *which*
position gets searched, so a check of the answer proves almost nothing — the
guard reads the wire.

## 6. The codebase's own comments as a coverage map — 2 defects

A comment explaining a past bug marks the exact line whose removal is invisible
in review. Grepping for "without this" and "used to" found both of this pass's
last defects, and as many again that turned out already covered.

- **The evaluation bar was a Pro reading.** "The column used to go with that
  switch" — turning off the win/draw/loss detail took the bar with it. The suite
  had never loaded with `showWdl: false`.
- **Chords belonged to the app.** `isPlainShortcut` exists because Command+F
  flipped the board and swallowed Find. The rule was unit-tested; nothing
  checked the handler still asks it, and the suite had never pressed a chord
  other than Ctrl+K.

## 7. Messages that are finished, grammatical, and about the wrong thing — 4 defects

A message only has to be *true* to survive review. Four were true and useless,
and the test for all four is the same: read it as somebody holding the thing it
is about, and ask what they do next.

- **A database that failed said nothing in the app's own words.** The browser's
  own exception reached the card, which is a sentence about IndexedDB written
  for whoever wrote IndexedDB.
- **A chess.com fetch that failed blamed the player.** "No games found for that
  player" is what the app says when the archive is empty, and it said it when
  the archive had never been read — a network error, a rate limit and a typo in
  a username all arriving as the same accusation.
- **A historical game that would not load blamed the reader's move numbers.**
  "Check the move text, headers, and move numbers" is right for somebody who
  pasted a game and wrong for somebody who pressed Load on a game they have
  never seen. **Measured** by answering the game fetch with a login page, which
  is what a captive portal sends.
- **A wrong file in the library importer pointed nowhere.** "That file is not a
  web-chess library backup" is true, and leaves a reader holding a file they
  have been told nothing about, in an app with three import controls that all
  take a file and two of which would have accepted it. The review importer next
  door already named all three; this one now says the same thing.

The paste boxes came out clean, which is worth recording: `pastedText.ts` exists
for exactly this, and a FEN in the game box and a game in the FEN box are each
named rather than called broken.

## 8. A switch, or a reset, read in one place and not another — 3 defects

State that governs something on the screen has to be read where the thing is
drawn, not only where it is decided. And a handler that starts a fresh game has
to clear what its siblings clear.

- **A hint said where to look when nothing was drawn.** With "Show board arrow
  overlays" off — the switch is three lines above the Hint button, in the same
  card — the hint still read "Try e4 — drawn on the board in green". The app
  handles that switch correctly everywhere else, which is what made the sentence
  worth looking for: the arrow-count control disables itself behind "Turn on
  board arrows first", and the reader's own drawn arrows are deliberately *not*
  gated on it, with a note saying why.
- **Switching mistake nudges off left the one on the screen.** The move loop
  reads the switch and decides whether a nudge is ever made; the card already
  drawn was a second place that had to read it, and did not — so it stayed,
  take-back button and all, answering a reader who had just asked for this to
  stop.
- **A new game did not get its own offer of a review.** "Review this game" is
  offered until it has run, and what decides "has run" is the frozen report.
  Four of the five handlers that start a fresh game clear it;
  `playFromCurrentPosition` was the fifth. Take a position into a game against
  the engine, play it out, bring it back to Analysis, and the offer was already
  spent by a game that was no longer on the board. The other four things its
  siblings clear were checked one at a time rather than copied across, and each
  is already cleared by an effect that handler trips.

Writing the first of these exposed a check that could not fail.
`checkAHintBelongsToItsPosition` asserted that a hint "drew an arrow" by
counting `svg line` across the whole document. **Measured** with the board
arrows switched off: 21 of them, none on the board — graph and bar strokes —
against 0 `svg line` inside the board and 1 `path[marker-end]`, which is what
the arrow actually is.

## 9. A cache key that does not match the request it keys — 1 defect

Three clients in this app cache a Lichess response: the tablebase, the cloud
evaluation and the opening explorer. Two key by exactly the parameters their
URL carries. The third did not.

- **A Masters request was keyed by fields it does not send.** The Masters
  database has no speeds and no ratings, and `buildUrl` leaves both off that
  request, so two Masters requests differing only there are the same request on
  the wire. The key was built from every field whatever the source, so changing
  a filter that Masters ignores counted as a different position and sent a
  second request to a rate-limited service for an answer already in hand. A key
  *finer* than its request is the quieter half of the usual cache bug -- it
  costs a fetch rather than serving a wrong answer -- which is how it survives
  review.

Recorded honestly: the app cannot reach it. Every call site strips both fields
for Masters and the explorer's controls are hidden for that source. It is the
module agreeing with itself, measured through its public surface -- two Masters
fetches differing only in those fields, asserted to make one request, two before
the fix.

## 10. Settings the suite has never exercised — 1 defect

Every persisted setting was listed and counted against the browser suite.
Nine had no coverage at all: the move sounds, the mistake nudges, the three
opening filters, the three advanced search limits, and the top-arrow count.
A setting nothing exercises is a setting whose default nobody has looked at.

- **The top-arrow slider promised arrows the board cannot draw.** The arrows
  are the engine's lines, sliced: `ranked.slice(0, topMoveArrowCount)` over
  the lines the engine returned. So the board draws the smaller of MultiPV and
  the top-arrow count, and the slider shows the larger. Out of the box they
  disagree -- `multiPv` defaults to 2 and `topMoveArrowCount` to 3 -- so a
  reader who has changed nothing has a control reading 3 above a board drawing
  2, and dragging it to 5 changes nothing. **Measured** with a fixture
  answering two lines. Said rather than clamped: the count is remembered, and
  a slider that rewrote itself when MultiPV dropped would lose a setting the
  reader chose.

The other eight were checked and are correctly wired. Two of those checks are
worth having anyway, because each settles a claim this pass had only read:
that the opening filters reach the request and that Masters is asked without
them -- which is what makes section 9's trap latent rather than live -- and
that all six advanced search limits arrive in the `go`. Both were proved
capable of failing before they were kept.

## The suite's own defects — 4

The safety net is code, and the same premises find bugs in it. These were
found while checking the app, and each one had been quietly weakening a check
that looked green.

- **A check that could not fail.** `checkAHintBelongsToItsPosition` asserted
  the hint "drew an arrow" by counting `svg line` across the whole document.
  **Measured** with board arrows switched off: 21 of them, none on the board.
  It now counts `path[marker-end]` inside the board, which is what the arrow
  is.
- **An export read that slept instead of waiting.** Three checks opened the
  PGN dialog with two fixed sleeps and returned `''` when either was short,
  which reads downstream as "the game exported nothing" and blames the app.
  It failed in WebKit for the new take-back clock check while the identical
  idiom passed in its sibling on the same engine.
- **A board measured mid-resize.** `checkReadingSpace` slept 400ms after
  changing the root font size. **Measured** by taking the sleep to zero:
  "clipped 14.1, width 542", where 542 is the board's 1x width. That is the
  flake this file records for it. The 400ms is kept as a floor and the check
  now waits for the geometry to stop moving.
- **A suite that asked Lichess for real.** The engine fixture promises that
  deterministic checks do not depend on Lichess uptime and guarded on
  `hostname === 'lichess.org'`, so `explorer.lichess.org` and
  `tablebase.lichess.org` both reached the network. Three checks papered over
  it with `context.route`, which intercepts in Chromium only -- the app
  reaches these through a service worker -- and `checkOpeningLayout` typed a
  token with no stub at all. Firefox said it out loud: the card read "Opening
  Explorer rejected the Lichess API token", a 401 from the actual service.
  The fixture now 404s every `lichess.org` host and five checks answer in the
  page instead, three of which had been Chromium-only because nobody had run
  them anywhere else.

## Written, measured, thrown away

- **A correction for the Firefox dialog clip.** Focusing a control scrolls the
  panel to its maximum and the control is fully inside — then the panel scrolls
  back 136px on its own, with no resize, no further focus event and no
  application frame on the stack. Two corrections were tried, 4/4 failing became
  1/4 and then 3/5, samples too small to tell apart. Scroll anchoring was the
  obvious suspect and is not it. Recorded rather than guessed at again.
- **Clearing a consumed share hash.** The hash stops describing the board the
  moment a move is played. `replaceState` fixes that and breaks the commoner
  case: reloading a link someone sent you then shows an empty board.
- **A rule that every disabled button carries a title.** It flagged two whose
  reason is the empty field beside them. A check that needs exceptions on its
  first run is measuring the wrong thing.
- **A sweep held by a request for a position nobody is looking at.** Two pieces
  of state name a position the engine still owes an answer for; the import
  sweep refuses to start while either is set; and the auto-analyze effect only
  consumes one when it matches the board. So a ponder request made by
  navigating, in a window where that effect declines to run, looked like it
  could name a position the board had left and hold the sweep for the session.
  **Measured: it cannot.** `clearImportSweep` clears the ponder request itself
  and every path that queues a sweep calls it, so the request cannot outlive the
  queue it would have blocked. The run written to catch the stall swept 34/40
  and then 35/40 across a review the board walked out of. The check survives as
  coverage of a feature that had none, retitled to say what it actually
  measures.

## Verified against the real engine

Everything above was found and fixed against the suite's fake engine, which is
a deliberate seam -- it makes searches deterministic and keeps 7MB of WASM out
of every run -- and it means none of it had been seen by real Stockfish.
`smoke-engines-browser.cjs` was run afterwards for that reason, at both widths
in all three browsers: **six runs, eighteen checks each, all passed**, with a
real multithreaded Stockfish 18 Lite, `isolated: true` everywhere, and a
position score of +0.34 in Chromium and Firefox against +0.38 in WebKit, which
is two engines disagreeing by a rounding rather than anything to look at.

The checks it covers are the ones the fixture cannot: a real UCI search, score
isolation, producer identity, a full game review at depth 6 and again deepened,
saving and reopening a review, a complete backup and restore, and a repetition
review of eight depth-10 searches. Worth knowing that this script has always
taken all three browsers and needs no argument to do it.

What it does *not* cover is Play mode, which is where this pass's clock work
is -- so four clock fixes had been asserted against a fixture whose search is
instant and whose boot is a constructor, and never watched against a real one.
`smoke-play-clock-browser.cjs` is the Play-mode sibling: one timed move against
real Stockfish, in all three engines.

| | boot | search | Black's clock | spent |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chromium | 137ms | 1330ms | 3:00 → 3:00 | 2s |
| Firefox | 563ms | 1397ms | 3:00 → 3:00 | 2s |
| WebKit | 132ms | 1214ms | 3:00 → 3:00 | 2s |

Black is still on 3:00 after the boot and 3:00 again after its move -- two
seconds of search returned by the two-second increment -- and the export reads
0:03:00 against a face of 3:00 in every engine. Firefox's 563ms boot is the
number worth keeping: four times the others, and every millisecond of it used
to come off the engine's clock in a game the reader had already started.
